About you
Tell me about yourself (about 90 seconds)
I'm a biomedical engineer who found my passion in data. I started in neurotechnology research, where we collected brain-signal data and analyzed it to find biomarkers. That's where I realized I enjoy building the systems around the data as much as the analysis.
At RYR, a construction materials company, I led the modernization of their data: I designed a MySQL database, built a Python app for data capture, and automated ETL and reporting.
Today I'm a data engineer at TCS, working on data pipelines for a large banking client. I started in production support and moved into a data engineer role, and now I'm the point of contact for two applications. I've built pipeline changes that reached production, including a new platform integration delivered in five weeks, and I drove the retirement of more than 800 components of a legacy application. I present these changes directly to the client, and I've received very positive feedback from them.
Now I'm looking for a role where I can work with a modern data stack, like dbt and cloud warehouses, take on more responsibility, and keep growing. That's why this role caught my attention.
What is your greatest strength?
Adapting quickly to new tools, teams and responsibilities. My biomedical background trained me to work between two worlds, doctors and engineers, translating needs from one side to the other. At TCS, I've changed teams several times, each with different technologies, dynamics and responsibilities, and each time I got up to speed quickly. That's how I became the point of contact for my own applications. For example, I had to learn Snowflake fast to validate the data of a cycle I was automating.
What is your greatest weakness?
I sometimes want every detail of a process before I start working on it, so I ask a lot of questions, and teammates can find some of them unnecessary. What I've learned is that the goal isn't more questions, but the right questions, to the right person, at the right time. In a decommissioning project, I asked my team many questions, but I didn't confirm the scope directly with the client, and that was the question that really mattered. Now I research on my own first: I read the documentation and write my own notes on the process. Then I group my remaining questions into one conversation with the person who owns the answer, and I state my assumptions so I can start working while I wait for confirmation.
How would your manager or teammates describe you?
Adaptable, curious and methodical. They'd also say I'm approachable and persistent: I keep trying different approaches until something works. For example, I ran a cycle manually for months while I worked out how to automate it, until it finally ran on its own.
What are you most proud of in your career so far?
Adapting to an industry that was new to me. I came from biomedical engineering, and I went from production support to being the point of contact for two applications, delivering production changes and retiring more than 800 components of a legacy application, while keeping the quality of my work consistent.
Motivation
Why Paylocity?
Three reasons. First, the impact: payroll data decides whether people get paid correctly and on time, and data integrity is something I already care about in my work with banking data. Second, the challenge: Paylocity is bringing HR, payroll and finance together on one platform, and that kind of data integration is exactly what I enjoy. Third, growth: I want to move from consulting to a product company, where I can own the data long term, and the team works with technologies I want to master, like dbt and cloud warehouses. I also like the people-first culture and the company's growth, including here in Guadalajara.
Why this role, and why data engineering?
I enjoy being the person who makes data reliable: designing how it's stored, moved and validated so other people can trust it. Over time, I want to understand the full data lifecycle, from ingestion to how analysts and the business use the data, because that makes me a better data engineer.
Why are you leaving TCS?
I've learned a lot at TCS: the processes of a critical banking project, how large teams work, and the discipline that sensitive data requires. Now I'm ready for more responsibility and a more modern data stack. My project relies mostly on legacy technology, and the security restrictions of the client environment make it difficult to move to other projects or technologies. So I'm looking for a company where I can keep growing with current tools.
Where do you see yourself in 3 to 5 years?
As a senior data engineer, leading technical decisions and, eventually, a team. I want to grow from the ground up, understanding how teams really work, so that one day I can build a team that supports each other, acts with integrity and delivers excellent work. Along the way, I want to keep improving processes and bring new technologies and practices into the team.
What motivates you at work?
Learning and seeing progress, both mine and the team's. I especially enjoy organizing people and resources to face a challenge together, and seeing everyone grow professionally in the process.
How you work
How do you handle pressure or tight deadlines?
I work well under pressure because I plan first. I list everything needed to complete the requirement, break it into steps and set time for each. When we had five weeks to integrate a new platform, with 6 new jobs, 2 new scripts and Informatica changes, planning that way is what let us deliver on time. I also rely on the team: when we support each other, pressure becomes manageable.
How do you prioritize when you have several urgent tasks?
First, I list everything that needs to be done. Then I prioritize by impact: what affects the business or downstream teams, what has a fixed deadline, and what is blocking other people. I plan the work in time, and if something doesn't fit, I tell my manager or stakeholders early and agree on the trade-off. When it helps, I ask for support to understand a process faster or to split the work, so downstream teams aren't affected.
What kind of manager or environment brings out your best?
Managers who communicate clearly and are patient enough to help their team grow. I also do my best in environments where people help each other. I'm approachable and I like helping teammates who need it, so we deliver our best work and improve our skills along the way.
How do you like to receive feedback?
In a call or in person, so I can ask questions and fully understand what I need to improve. I appreciate it when it's early and specific. For feedback about how the team works, I like discussing it together, for example in a retrospective, so the whole team improves its communication.

I keep looking for courses and training. For example, I completed dbt training, and I built a machine learning project on my own to predict cardiovascular disease. Technical thought process
Walk me through the architecture of your current pipeline.
Every month we receive hundreds of thousands of records from mainframe sources. They go through a mainframe file platform that records and controls the files, then through Informatica PowerCenter for transformation, and they land in Snowflake. Control-M orchestrates the jobs, the pipeline code lives in Bash scripts managed in GitLab, and data reaches downstream consumers through SFTP. My role is to validate data integrity, coordinate with upstream and downstream teams so the application runs correctly, and adapt the application to the client's new needs, like the recent platform integration.
How do you make sure the data you deliver is correct?
First, I check volumes: that the number of records matches what we expected. Then I verify integrity by comparing the output with the source data, using counts and totals. I confirm with upstream teams what data was expected, and with downstream teams that they received it correctly, and I report any findings. I also built traceability reports that make it faster to trace and resolve errors.

I've worked with Snowflake and Python, and I completed dbt training and used it in some work, so I want to go deeper with dbt. I haven't used Redshift or BigQuery yet, but they're cloud data warehouses like Snowflake, columnar and massively parallel, so many concepts transfer and I'd learn the specifics quickly.
What technology would you like to learn next?
AWS and modern cloud warehouses. Most modern data platforms run in the cloud, and my current project relies mostly on legacy tools, so it's the natural next step for my career.
